About ogham

Ogham is an alphabet of strokes cut across a stemline, on the edges of standing stones in Ireland and western Britain from roughly the fourth to the sixth century, for an early form of Irish. There are twenty letters in four groups of five, called aicmí. A stone is read from the bottom up.

This tool is a transliterator, not a translator. It maps the letters of a modern spelling, one for one, to ogham letters. That is a modern convenience rather than how the stones ever spelled anything, but it lets a word survive a round trip back to Latin letters.

The choices made here

Fadas are dropped, because ogham did not write vowel length. The letter h after a consonant (bh, ch, dh, fh, gh, mh, ph, sh, th) marks lenition in modern Irish, which ogham never wrote, so the stones give no guidance. This tool keeps that h as the letter Úath by default, and offers a historical "drop" for a cleaner look.

Sounds Irish lacks are mapped by convention and flagged: k becomes Coll, v and w become Fearn, j and y become Iodhadh, x becomes Coll plus Sail. The letter p is written with Pín, the double-X sign the stones themselves added for it (Cool East, Crickhowell and Margam; Cool East cuts it as a single X because the double form is hard to carve). The older Q substitution and the later manuscript letter Peith are offered as alternatives. P is never silently turned into b.

The feather mark

A chevron at the foot of the stem, pointing toward the letters, marks where the writing begins. There is no closing mark by default: the OG(H)AM project advises against one for modern use, so it is offered only as an option.

Two traditions

On stone, vowels were cut as short notches across the edge. In the later manuscripts they are drawn as full strokes on a stemline, which is the default here. The Monumental setting switches the vowels back to notches. It changes the vowels only and does not pretend to be a stone.

Why it is drawn, not typed

Ogham has a Unicode block, but every font draws it differently, most render only horizontally, and none let you set the stroke length, spacing or physical size. Here every letter is drawn as SVG from its geometry, so the output is exact, scalable, and ready for a tattoo artist, a laser cutter or a page.

The ink

The colourways are grounded in real Irish material: the pigments of the Book of Kells, the dyes of early Irish cloth, the enamel of La Tène metalwork. Carved black is the default, because black on white is what a stencil or a laser cutter needs.
